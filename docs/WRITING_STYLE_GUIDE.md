# Style Guide: Writing That Reads as Human
### Derived from Wikipedia's *Signs of AI Writing* — For Academic Research Paper Use

---

> **How to use this guide:** Each principle identifies a pattern that AI writing commonly exhibits and that you should consciously avoid or revise. The guide is organized by category so you can work through your paper section by section.

---

## Category 1: Content & Substance

### 1.1 Avoid "Regression to the Mean" — Be Specific, Not Generic
**The AI tendency:** LLMs statistically gravitate toward the most common, broadly applicable descriptions. They replace specific, unusual facts with sweeping, generic statements that could apply to almost anything. The highly particular gets smoothed into the bland and universal.

**Principle:** Every claim you make should be *only* true of *this* topic. If a sentence could be lifted from a paper on a completely different subject with minimal changes, it is too generic. Replace vague characterizations with concrete data, named studies, specific dates, precise mechanisms, or domain-specific terminology.

- ❌ Generic: "This represents a significant contribution to our understanding of the field."
- ✓ Specific: "This finding revises the 2019 consensus model by demonstrating that [X mechanism] operates at temperatures below 4°C."

---

### 1.2 Do Not Inflate Significance, Legacy, and "Broader Implications"
**The AI tendency:** AI writing pads out text with unearned commentary about how something is "groundbreaking," "revolutionary," "seminal," or "transformative." It inserts whole sentences or paragraphs devoted to asserting importance rather than demonstrating it.

**Principle:** Let the evidence speak. Do not add a sentence proclaiming the importance of a finding; the substance of the finding itself should convey that. Remove boilerplate phrases like "this has profound implications for," "represents a paradigm shift in," or "is reshaping the landscape of."

- ❌ AI: "This study's findings have far-reaching implications for the broader field and will undoubtedly shape future research directions."
- ✓ Human: [State the finding and allow the reader to draw their own conclusions about scope.]

---

### 1.3 Avoid "Canned" Notability and Media Coverage Language
**The AI tendency:** AI frequently signals importance by referencing reception, media coverage, and widespread recognition — even when that is not the point — using phrases like "widely regarded as," "has received widespread attention," or "has been noted by scholars and practitioners alike."

**Principle:** In academic writing, significance is established by citing specific studies and results, not by asserting that something is widely covered. Remove any language that substitutes reception for argument.

---

### 1.4 Do Not End with Outline-Like "Challenges and Future Prospects"
**The AI tendency:** AI-generated conclusions reliably follow a formula: name 2–3 challenges, then name 2–3 future directions, in a neat list. The section reads like the summary section of a slide deck, not like a thoughtful synthesis.

**Principle:** Your conclusion should grow organically from the argument you have made. If you discuss limitations, tie them to specific methodological decisions already explained in the paper. If you identify future directions, anchor them to specific gaps your findings reveal — not to a generic list of "open questions in the field."

---

### 1.5 Avoid Superficial Analysis
**The AI tendency:** AI describes things rather than analyzes them. It summarizes the surface of a topic without engaging with tensions, contradictions, methodological debates, or unsettled questions. It produces coverage without depth.

**Principle:** For each major claim in your paper, ask: *What does this tension or contradiction reveal? What cannot yet be resolved, and why?* Superficial writing presents consensus; analytical writing interrogates it.

---

## Category 2: Language & Vocabulary

### 2.1 Eliminate "AI Vocabulary" — The Overused Word List
**The AI tendency:** Certain words appear at abnormally high density in LLM output. Their presence in clusters is a reliable indicator of AI generation. These words are not wrong individually — they become a problem through overuse and through substituting for more precise language.

**Avoid overusing:**
- *delve, delves into* — replace with "examines," "analyzes," "investigates"
- *nuance(d), nuances* — use specific contrasts instead of labeling them
- *tapestry* — avoid this metaphor entirely in academic writing
- *multifaceted* — specify the facets
- *encompass, encompassing*
- *realm, landscape, ecosystem* (as abstract metaphors)
- *robust* (except in technical statistical contexts where it has a precise meaning)
- *holistic, comprehensive, meticulous* — these are filler descriptors
- *pivotal, groundbreaking, transformative, revolutionary*
- *leverage* (as a verb meaning "to use")
- *underscore, underscores* — replace with "shows," "demonstrates," "reveals"
- *foster, fostering*
- *navigate(s)*
- *intricate*
- *shed light on*
- *stands as, serve as*
- *it is worth noting that* — just note it
- *it is important to* — just state the importance directly
- *in the realm of*
- *at its core*
- *in today's [fast-paced/rapidly evolving/interconnected] world*

**Principle:** Scan your draft for these words. Each time one appears, ask whether there is a more precise alternative. Reducing their density is not about prohibition — it is about precision.

---

### 2.2 Restore Simple Copulatives ("is / are")
**The AI tendency:** AI avoids the verb *to be*. It replaces "X is Y" with more elaborate verb constructions that add no meaning: "X functions as," "X serves as," "X operates as," "X stands as," "X acts as." This makes prose sound performatively formal.

**Principle:** In academic writing, simple declarative sentences with *is* and *are* are clear and authoritative. Do not dress up a simple statement of definition or identity with a fancier verb.

- ❌ "This framework serves as a foundational lens through which scholars examine..."
- ✓ "This framework is a standard tool for analyzing..."

---

### 2.3 Break Up Negative Parallelisms
**The AI tendency:** AI is strongly attracted to constructions that contrast two things using a negative:
- "Not just X, but also Y" (used repeatedly)
- "Not X, but Y"
- "X rather than Y" (used at high frequency as a structuring device)

These are legitimate constructions, but AI overuses them as a way of creating a sense of depth without actually adding information.

**Principle:** Audit your draft for all instances of "not just... but also," "not X but Y," and "[X] rather than [Y]." Some uses are appropriate; if they cluster, break them up. Prefer direct positive assertions over contrast-as-argument.

---

### 2.4 Resist the "Rule of Three"
**The AI tendency:** AI lists things in groups of exactly three almost reflexively. Three is statistically the most common list length in LLM output. Sentences like "X is efficient, effective, and adaptable" or introductions listing "three key themes" often signal templated thinking.

**Principle:** Let the logic of your argument determine list length. Two items may be sufficient; four may be more accurate. The rule of three is a rhetorical heuristic for speeches and essays — academic writing should be governed by the actual number of items that exist, not by a default of three.

---

### 2.5 Avoid "Elegant Variation" — Unnecessary Synonym-Hunting
**The AI tendency:** To avoid repeating words, AI cycles through synonyms: a study becomes "the research," then "the investigation," then "the analysis," then "the scholarly inquiry." This is called *elegant variation* and it creates ambiguity — in academic writing, a reader may wonder whether different words signal different things.

**Principle:** In academic writing, it is acceptable — often preferable — to repeat key terms consistently. Consistent terminology aids precision. Only vary a term when the variant genuinely means something different.

## Category 3: Sentence & Syntax Patterns

### 3.1 Vary Sentence Complexity Naturalistically
**The AI tendency:** LLM output tends toward uniform sentence complexity — typically medium-length, structurally similar sentences with similar rhythms. Human academic writing naturally varies: some sentences are short and declarative, some are long and elaborately subordinated, some are fragments used for emphasis.

**Principle:** After drafting a paragraph, read it aloud. If all sentences have a similar beat and length, deliberately introduce variation. A short sentence after a long one creates emphasis. An occasional fragment is acceptable in academic writing when it serves emphasis.

---

### 3.2 Do Not Use Rhetorical Framing as Filler
**The AI tendency:** AI pads transitions and introductions with meta-commentary: "In order to fully understand X, it is first necessary to examine Y." "Before we can address Z, we must consider..." "Having established X, we can now turn to Y." These phrases are often empty — the substance should simply be stated.

**Principle:** Remove transitional throat-clearing. If a new section begins with a sentence explaining what the section is about to do, ask whether that sentence can be cut and the actual substance started immediately.

---

## Category 4: Structure & Formatting

### 4.1 Do Not Over-Bold
**The AI tendency:** AI frequently bolds phrases throughout body text to create the appearance of a structured, scannable document. In academic papers, this reads as inappropriate and amateurish.

**Principle:** Bold text in academic writing is reserved for technical terms upon first definition (in some disciplines) or for specific editorial conventions. Do not bold for emphasis in running prose.

---

### 4.2 Use Running Prose Over Lists
**The AI tendency:** AI converts argument into bulleted or numbered lists. This is appropriate for genuinely enumerative content (methods, criteria, data), but AI uses it to structure content that would be better rendered as prose with subordinate clauses and connective logic.

**Principle:** If a list of three or four items represents an argument rather than a classification, write it as a sentence or paragraph. Lists suppress the logical connectives (*because, therefore, however, although*) that show how ideas relate. Academic argument needs those connectives visible.

---

### 4.3 Avoid Em-Dash Overuse
**The AI tendency:** AI uses em-dashes at high frequency — often several times per paragraph — to append explanations or qualifications to sentences. The em-dash is an informal punctuation mark; its overuse makes academic prose feel casual and unedited.

**Principle:** In academic writing, em-dashes should be used sparingly for strong parenthetical interruptions. Most uses can be replaced by a comma, a colon, or a restructured clause.

---

## Category 5: Tone & Register

### 5.1 Eliminate Vague Attribution and Opinion Laundering
**The AI tendency:** AI attributes views to unnamed, unspecified entities: "many scholars argue," "critics have noted," "experts believe," "some have suggested." This vague attribution allows AI to state opinions while appearing to cite evidence.

**Principle:** Every attributed view in academic writing must be traceable to specific sources. "Many scholars argue X" should become "Smith (2018), Chen and Park (2021), and Okonkwo (2023) argue X." If you cannot identify specific sources for a claim, do not make the claim.

---

### 5.2 Do Not Use Promotional Language
**The AI tendency:** AI text frequently reads like advertising copy or a press release: admiring adjectives, an optimistic frame, and the implicit assumption that the subject being described is inherently worthy of praise.

**Principle:** Academic writing is dispassionate by convention. Describe results, methods, and findings accurately. Reserve evaluative language for situations where you have evidence to support an evaluation, and match the strength of your language to the strength of that evidence.

---

### 5.3 Maintain Consistent Register Throughout
**The AI tendency:** AI-generated text sometimes shifts registers inconsistently — formal in one paragraph, colloquial in the next. This inconsistency can occur when different parts of a paper were generated separately.

**Principle:** Academic writing maintains a consistent formal register. Read your paper from start to finish specifically to check for shifts in register: sudden informalities, contractions, casual expressions, or unexplained shifts in vocabulary level.

---

## Category 6: Markers of Human Writing — What to Preserve and Cultivate

The Wikipedia article also identifies characteristics that signal *human* authorship. These are worth actively cultivating:

### 6.1 Syntactic Idiosyncrasy
Human writing has irregular rhythms, unexpected sentence structures, and distinctive syntactic habits. These irregularities — which trained writers sometimes call "voice" — are difficult to generate statistically and are reliable markers of human authorship.

**Principle:** Do not edit for perfect uniformity. Some idiosyncrasy in sentence construction is authentic and should be preserved, provided it does not compromise clarity.

---

### 6.2 Ability to Justify Editorial Choices
Human writers can explain *why* they made the choices they made — why they used a particular term, why a particular study was cited, why an argument was structured in a particular order.

**Principle:** As you write, be able to articulate the rationale for each major claim, each citation, and each structural choice. This is not just a style concern — it is a scholarly integrity concern.

---

### 6.3 Specificity Over Generality
Human experts write about their field with specificity that statistical models cannot easily reproduce: naming specific instruments, citing specific papers by year and author, using precise technical vocabulary that has narrow meaning within the discipline.

**Principle:** Treat precision as the primary virtue. When you notice a claim is general enough to appear in any paper on the topic, make it more specific until it is only true of *your* study's context.

---

## Quick-Reference Checklist

Before submitting your revised paper, scan for:

- [ ] "AI vocabulary" words: *delve, nuanced, tapestry, robust, transformative, leverage, underscore, foster, multifaceted, it is worth noting, in the realm of*
- [ ] "Not just X, but also Y" — used more than once or twice
- [ ] Sentences where *is/are* was replaced with *serves as / functions as / stands as*
- [ ] Unattributed opinions: *many scholars argue, experts believe, critics note*
- [ ] Significance-inflating language: *groundbreaking, pivotal, paradigm shift, profound implications*
- [ ] Lists of exactly three items that could be condensed or expanded
- [ ] Boilerplate conclusions: challenge 1, challenge 2, future direction 1, future direction 2
- [ ] Excessive em-dashes (more than 1–2 per page)
- [ ] Bold text in body prose
- [ ] Paragraphs that describe without analyzing
